iPrivacy

SigmaPro runs entirely inside your browser. The data you open, the analyses and the reports are computed on your computer and are never sent to any server, not even for the models that run in the R engine, which also works inside the browser. You can analyze the unpublished data of a thesis or a trial without them ever leaving your machine.

I.1What is SigmaPro?

SigmaPro is a statistics program for agronomic and biological research that runs inside the browser, with nothing to install and no internet connection. It takes the data of a trial along a single path, from the field file to the published table and figure.

That path is the path of the blocks: load the data, explore them, choose the analysis that matches the design, read the conclusion, check the assumptions and export the report. It is made for people who analyze their own experiments—undergraduate and graduate students, teachers, field and greenhouse researchers—and want correct results without writing code. That is why each result begins with a conclusion in words, checks its assumptions, cites its methods and gives the R code that repeats the calculation. Its 78 analyses, in 18 families, range from the analysis of variance of an experimental design to mixed models, heritability, multi-environment trials, survival and time series. Each one has its Fundamentals fact sheet, which explains what it does, what it assumes, what data it needs and how to read it (chapter 9).

And every number is checked against R: the program’s computing core is its own, but R is the judge. The comparison, case by case, comes inside the app (chapter 8). SigmaPro is part of the LABG Suite, a set of free apps that also run in the browser; chapter 10 explains which of them continue the work of an analysis.

I.3Five ideas behind the manual

1. The design comes first

The analysis of an experiment depends on how it was carried out: whether there were blocks, whether a factor was applied to large plots, whether the measurements were repeated on the same plant. SigmaPro reads the structure of the trial from the data (section 2.13), and the assistant asks about what cannot be read, so that it proposes the analysis that matches the design, not the best-known one.

2. The conclusion first, in words

Each result begins by saying what it found (“hibrido: there are differences (F = 60.635, p < 0.001)”) and then gives the tables. The numbers are there for anyone who wants to check them; the conclusion is there so that nobody misreads them.

3. Assumptions are not taken for granted

The analyses check their own assumptions: most of them show the check with a traffic light in the result, and the experimental designs check them with Check the model assumptions (section 4.7). If something fails, the app says what to do: transform, change the test or use a model that does not make that assumption.

4. Every number is validated against R

A method does not appear in the app until all its validation cases agree with R to six digits (chapter 8).

5. Everything can be reproduced

Each result comes with its R code, the report cites the methods with their versions, and the .labg project saves the data and the analyses with their fingerprints, so that another person arrives at the same numbers.

I.4Data it accepts and what it delivers

SigmaPro reads tables in delimited text (CSV, TSV), spreadsheet workbooks (.xlsx, .ods) and pasted tables, with one row per experimental unit and one column per variable (section 2.2). It recognizes factors, numeric variables, dates and identifiers, and lets you correct any of them. It works comfortably with about 100,000 rows.

It delivers the complete report in .docx, PDF or HTML; each table in CSV, and all of them together in a spreadsheet or in LaTeX; the figures in SVG, vector PDF, PNG or TIFF at 600 dpi; and the .labg project with the data, the analyses and the R code. In addition, each Fundamentals fact sheet gives a template in CSV or XLSX for preparing your data (section 9.5). Appendix A describes each file in detail.

I.5What you need and how to open it

  • A recent browser, from the last two or three years, on a computer; it also works on a tablet or a phone, with the screen rearranged (section I.6).
  • Nothing to install. SigmaPro opens with a double-click on its index.html file, from the program folder, or from its web page. The first time the web page is opened, the browser stores the app so that it can be used offline afterward.
  • Internet only for the R engine, the first time you use an analysis that needs it (section I.7). Nothing else uses the internet.

The opening screen

When you open SigmaPro, its opening screen appears: on a black background, the logo and the name are drawn in about six seconds, and the scene stays still until you press Start (or Enter). Skip or Esc dismisses it at any time.

  • The first time, it is shown in full; on later visits, a short version of a second and a half, also with Start. Reloading the same tab does not show it again.
  • If the system asks for reduced motion, the scene appears still.
  • It can be turned off, replayed or projected in a loop from Home → About SigmaPro (chapter 1).

Spanish or English

The ES · EN selector on the top bar changes the language of the whole app: the menus, the conclusions, the tables, the report, the methods paragraph and the Fundamentals fact sheets. When you change it, the app saves the work and reloads in the other language, with the same data and the same analyses; the chosen language is remembered the next time. The comments in the equivalent R code stay in Spanish. This manual describes the English interface.

I.7The R engine inside the browser

Almost all of SigmaPro runs on its own computing core, written for the program. Some methods—linear mixed models, repeated measures and generalized linear mixed models—need algorithms that R handles better than anything else, and for them SigmaPro includes an R engine: R compiled to run inside the browser (webR), with the packages lme4, lmerTest, emmeans, nlme and car.

  • The first time you use one of those methods, the app tells you and downloads the engine (about 25 MB). After that, it stays stored in the browser and works without internet.
  • The data do not leave your machine: the engine runs on your own computer, like the rest of the app.
  • When the app is published with its motor-r/ folder and opened from a server, the engine is loaded from that same folder, and no internet is needed, not even the first time.

Section 4.6 explains the notice and which methods use the engine.

I.8How to work through a study

The order of the blocks is the order in which a trial is analyzed. You do not have to go through all of them: whatever a block needs from another, it takes on its own.

  1. Block 2. Load the data and check the type of each variable; read what SigmaPro sees in the trial: the design, the missing values, the empty cells.
  2. Block 3. Explore: means, spread and odd values by treatment, before testing anything.
  3. Block 4. Choose the analysis with the assistant (or in the catalog, or build the model in the model builder); read the conclusion and the traffic light; compare the means; add to the report what you want to keep.
  4. Block 5. Review the report: the order, the titles, the notes and the paragraph for the thesis.
  5. Block 6. Adjust the figures: the error bars, the width and, if needed, the ✎ editor or the Figure Studio.
  6. Block 7. Export the report, the tables and the figures, and save the .labg project.

At any step, the ⓘ What is this analysis? button opens the analysis’s fact sheet in Fundamentals (Block 9), with what it assumes and the data it needs, and at the foot of some results the Continue in the LABG suite strip suggests the app that continues the work (Block 10).

I.9The examples

The Data block includes 27 example trials (table 2.2), one or more for each family of analyses, embedded in the program so that they work with a double-click. Six are published data—Mendel’s segregation, the poisons of Box and Cox (1964), the oats in split plots of Yates (1935), an alpha-lattice and an oat trial in 14 environments from Olivoto and Lúcio (2020), and the elongation of a yarn made from three polymers, from Cornell (2002)—and the rest are fictitious, built so that each analysis has a clear case. The main example of the manual is “Maize: hybrids × nitrogen”: 3 hybrids with 4 nitrogen rates in 4 randomized complete blocks, 48 plots.

I.10What the program does not do

SigmaPro does what it does well, and it says what it does not do yet. These are its known limits.

  • The manual and the interface are available only in Spanish and English.
  • The PDF of the report comes from the browser’s print dialog; in the .docx, the figures are embedded as PNG images at 300 dpi. The LaTeX is written ready to compile, but the app does not compile it.
  • The Data block does not average or sort rows: if you need the mean of each plot or the dates in order, prepare them in your spreadsheet before importing.
  • With two SigmaPro tabs open at the same time, autosave keeps the work of the last one that changed; the worksheet’s undo history is not saved.
  • With many rows, each scatter plot draws at most 2,500 points (the calculations use all of them).
  • Sampling: only simple random and stratified sampling (not cluster or systematic sampling).
  • Generalized linear mixed models: they use the Laplace approximation; Gauss-Hermite quadrature only with a single random intercept, in binomial or Poisson models.
  • Heritability: it does not handle fixed controls within a trial of random genotypes, or relationship matrices.
  • Multivariate: it includes MANOVA and discriminant analysis; principal components and cluster analysis are done in PCAPro and ClusteringPro, from the same suite (chapter 10).

1.2The three commitments

Below the paths, three cards sum up what sets SigmaPro apart. They are not slogans: each one can be checked; in the first, See the validation leads to Block 8 (chapter 8).

10⁻⁶
Verified accuracy
Each method is compared against R, with data from the literature and with cases built on purpose, before it is offered in the catalog. The rule is that no number may differ from R’s by more than one millionth (relative to its size). A method whose cases have not all passed does not appear in the app. Block 8 shows the outcome of those comparisons.
RReproducible

Every result comes with the R code that gives the same result, with the data, the model and the options that were used. It lets a reviewer, a thesis advisor or you yourself, years later, repeat the analysis without SigmaPro. The .labg project also stores a script that repeats all the analyses in the report (chapter 7).

0Private

Everything is computed in the browser: the data are not sent to any server, not even for the models that run in the R engine, which also works inside the browser (section I.7). Opened with a double-click, the app makes no request outside the computer, except for the first download of the R engine.

A difference of one millionth means that, in practice, SigmaPro’s figures and R’s are the same in every decimal place that gets published. An F of 60.635 in SigmaPro is an F of 60.635 in R; a p value of 0.0034 is 0.0034. When a method cannot reach that accuracy because R itself introduces larger numerical noise (for example, in certain iterative fits), the case carries its own tolerance with its reason written down, and that is how it appears in the validation (chapter 8).

2.7The five variable types

The type of a column decides how it enters the analyses. It is the most important decision in the block: a block written with numbers (1, 2, 3, 4) and taken as numeric turns a block analysis into a meaningless regression.

TypeWhat it isHow it enters the analyses
numericA measurement: yield, height, weight, count, percentage. Also a rate that you want to study as a curve.As a response or as a covariate. A numeric rate with few repeated values can also be used as a design factor (the analysis offers this).
factorA group: block, treatment, variety, lot, site, year of the trial.As a design factor; its levels have an order that decides the order of the means in tables and figures.
dateA date (sowing, transplanting, harvest), in YYYY-MM-DD or DD/MM/YYYY.To order time series and to compute days between dates; never as a design factor.

How the app proposes the type

When importing, SigmaPro proposes a type for each column and always explains why. The rules, in order:

  1. If all the values are numbers, the column is numeric, except in two cases: if they are integers, each row has a different one and the name suggests a key (parcela, id, folio, número, planta…), it is an identifier; if they are integers with up to 30 values that repeat and the name suggests a design factor (bloque, rep, tratamiento, variedad, lote, sitio, año…), it is a factor.
  2. If at least eight out of ten values are numbers and the rest are stray texts (“s/d”, “lost”), it is numeric and those texts become missing, with a warning.
  3. If all of them are dates, it is a date.
  4. If they are texts that repeat, it is a factor. If each row has a different one, it is an identifier when they look like codes (P01, P02…), when the name suggests a key or when there are more than 30 rows; otherwise, it is text.
!Always check numeric codes

If a column of integers with few values does not have a recognizable name (for example T or grupo2), the app leaves it as numeric and warns you: “These are numbers, but there are only 4 distinct repeated values: if they are group codes, change it to a factor”. It is worth reading the reason for each variable once, when you load the data.

The ⋮ menu of each column

  • Rename… changes the name (it cannot repeat an existing one).
  • Transform… (for numeric columns) opens the window of section 2.9.

The order of the levels

The order of the levels decides the order of the means in tables and figures, and which one is the reference level: the first one, against which the others are compared in a Dunnett test, in Cox hazard ratios or in the coefficients of a model. That is why it is a good idea to put the control first.

The Numeric, Alphabetical and As they appear buttons sort them all at once; the ↑ and ↓ arrows move one level. Apply saves the order (and it can be undone).

2.9Transforming a variable

When the residuals of an analysis do not have constant variance or are not normal (chapter 4, the assumptions), a transformation of the response often solves the problem. ↻ Transform… creates a new column with the transformed variable; the original does not change, so the two analyses can be compared.

TransformationWhen it helps
ln(x)Positive data whose standard deviation grows in proportion to the mean (weights, biomass, pathogen loads).
log₁₀(x)The same, in base 10 (easier to read in orders of magnitude).
ln(x + c)When there are zeros. You choose the constant c (1 by default).
√xCounts whose variance is proportional to the mean (Poisson distribution).
√(x + 0.5)Counts with zeros or with small values (insects, weeds, lesions).
asin(√p)Proportions and percentages (germination, incidence, mortality). The window detects whether the data are in percent (0 to 100) and lets you choose the result in degrees or in radians.

Box-Cox

The Box and Cox (1964) transformation is a family of powers, xλ, that includes the previous ones as special cases: λ = 1 leaves the data as they are, λ = 0.5 is the square root, λ = 0 the logarithm and λ = −1 the reciprocal. The value of λ is estimated by maximum likelihood from the residuals of the experiment’s model, not from the raw data: what must be stabilized is the variation within treatments, not the difference between them.

  1. Choose the variable and select Box-Cox.
  2. Check the factors (and the block) that will be used in the analysis. The app builds the model: blocks, rows and columns are added; treatments are crossed. In the example, tiempo ~ veneno * tratamiento.
  3. Estimate λ computes λ̂, its 95% confidence interval (the values of λ with 2[L(λ̂) − L(λ)] ≤ χ²0.95; 1 = 3.84) and the likelihood curve, with the maximum and the interval marked.
  4. The app suggests a simple power (none, square root, logarithm, reciprocal square root, reciprocal or square) if it falls within the interval, because it is easier to interpret than λ̂ and almost as good. The value to be used goes in the λ box, which you can change by hand.
  5. Create the column stores (xλ − 1)/λ (or ln x if λ = 0) under the name you choose; the details of the variable record the λ used.
iThe means of a transformed variable

The means and their differences are on the transformed scale. To present them in the original units, apply the inverse transformation to the means (for example, square the means of √x), bearing in mind that the result is a median, not a mean. An alternative that avoids the problem is generalized linear models (section 4.17), which model counts and proportions on their own scale.

2.10Filtering rows

Filter rows… leaves out of the analyses the rows that do not meet a condition, without deleting them. It is useful for analyzing a single site of a multi-site trial, removing a damaged block or excluding plots with a recorded problem. The condition is written in the notation of section 2.8, and the window tells you instantly how many rows pass.

With the filter active, a notice above the grid reminds you of it, with the condition and how many rows pass, and the Remove the filter button. The rows that do not pass remain in the grid, hatched and with their number struck through; you can still see and correct them.

Everything that comes afterward uses only the rows that pass: the summaries in the details, the diagnosis in section 2.13, Explore, Analysis and the report. The .labg project saves the filter condition.

Some useful conditions:

  • sitio == "Norte"
  • bloque != "4"
  • !is.na(rend)
  • hibrido %in% c("H-52", "H-66")
  • dosis > 0 & dosis < 180

A row whose result is missing (for example, rend > 5 with rend empty) does not pass. A filter that leaves no rows is not applied.

2.11Wide and long tables

A variable measured several times on the same unit is often entered in wide format: one row per plant and one column per date (altura_15dds, altura_30dds…). The analyses require the long format: one row per measurement, with one column that gives the date and another with the value. Wide ⇄ long… makes the change in both directions.

  1. In Wide → long, check the columns that identify each unit (those that will be repeated in each new row: here sustrato and planta) and the columns to stack (the measurements).
  2. Name the new column that will hold the names of the stacked columns (here dds, days after transplanting) and the values column (altura).
  3. If each stacked column has a number in its name, the Keep the number in each name checkbox keeps only that number (altura_15dds → 15), and the new column comes out numeric, ready for a curve over time; without the checkbox, it keeps the full name as a factor.
  4. Reshape loads the new table. The identifiers keep their type; a column that was an identifier (one plant per row) becomes a factor, because in the long table each plant is repeated: it is the subject of the repeated measures.

In Long → wide, choose the columns that identify each unit, the column whose values will name the new columns, and the column with the values. If a combination of identifiers is repeated, the app stops and explains why: the wide table would not know which value to use, and another column is needed to tell those rows apart.

2.12Undo, redo and go back

Every change to the sheet (a cell, a paste, a type, a new or deleted column, an order of levels) can be undone with ↶ Undo or Ctrl+Z, and redone with ↷ Redo or Ctrl+Y. Hovering the pointer over the buttons shows what is about to be undone (“Undo: Change ‘dosis’ to factor”).

Reshaping the table creates a new table, and so does loading the example data with “See it in action” from the glossary (section I.6). To return to the previous table there is the Back to the previous table button, which appears in the toolbar for as long as you can go back.

The main table gives, for each variable and group: n, mean, standard deviation (SD), standard error of the mean (SE), coefficient of variation (CV, in %), the confidence interval of the mean, the minimum, the quartiles Q1 and Q3, the median and the maximum. ΣThe formulas

With n observations of mean x̄ and standard deviation s:

SE = s / √n · CV = 100 s / x̄ · CI of the mean: x̄ ± t1−α/2; n−1 · SE

The interval of the median is formed by two ordered observations, x(k) and x(n−k+1), with k taken from the binomial distribution (n, ½); that is why its actual confidence (“Actual confidence (%)”) is the first one that reaches or exceeds the requested level, not exactly that level. The interval of the variance comes from the chi-square, (n − 1)s² / χ²; it assumes normality, and the interval of the SD is its square root.

The conclusions

Without groups, the conclusion summarizes the variable: “‘rend’ (n = 48): mean 5.938 (95% CI: 5.644 to 6.232), standard deviation 1.013 and coefficient of variation 17.1%.” With groups, it says which one has the highest mean and which the lowest. It also warns in two cases: when, in a group of eight or more observations, the skewness is greater than twice its standard error (a sign of outliers or that a transformation would help, section 2.9), and when some rows were left out because they have no level in a grouping factor.

iThe CV in agricultural experiments

The CV of a group describes the variation of the raw data; it mixes the variation among blocks with the error variation. The CV published for a trial is a different one: that of the analysis of variance, computed with the error mean square (in the maize example, 3.6% against the 16% of the raw data by hybrid). That one is given by the analysis of the design in the Analysis block.

3.4Frequency tables

They count how many observations there are at each level of a factor (Variable) or, with Cross with, at each combination of two. The Count missing values as a category checkbox adds a row and a column for the empty cells. Numeric columns with few values appear in the lists marked “(numeric)”.

A cross-tabulation has four views, chosen with its buttons: Frequencies, % by row (each row adds up to 100), % by column and % of total. 3.5Proportions and intervals

A proportion can be requested in two ways, chosen with the buttons above the options:

  • Categories of a factor: the proportion of each level of a categorical variable (for example, what fraction of the leaves has each rust score).
  • Successes out of trials: two numeric columns, one with the successes (germinated seeds, dead plants, damaged fruits) and one with the trials (seeds sown, plants observed). The rows are summed within each group.

By group computes one proportion per level of a factor, and Confidence sets the level of the intervals. Under Intervals you check the methods you want to see, one column each.

IntervalWhat it is like
WilsonInverts the score test (Wilson, 1927). Its actual coverage stays very close to the requested level even with few trials or extreme proportions. Recommended.
Wilson with continuity correctionWilson’s interval with a continuity correction: wider and conservative.
Clopper-Pearson (exact)Based on the beta distribution. It guarantees at least the requested confidence, at the price of being wider than necessary.

3.6Goodness of fit

The chi-square goodness-of-fit test tells whether some observed frequencies are consistent with an expected ratio. The classic case in agronomy is Mendelian segregation: in the F2 of a dihybrid, the phenotypes are expected in a 9:3:3:1 ratio.

OptionWhat it decides
CategoriesThe variable with the categories (the phenotypes).
CountsIf each row already gives how many observations its category has (as in Mendel’s example, with four rows), the column with the counts. If each row is one observation, leave it at “one row per observation”.
Expected proportionOne proportion per category, in the order of the levels shown in the help text below, separated by colons: 9:3:3:1, 3:1, 1:2:1. Decimals are also accepted (0.5625:0.1875:0.1875:0.0625). If left empty, equal proportions are tested.
Significance levelThe α the conclusion uses to decide (0.05, 0.01 or 0.10).

The table gives, for each category, the observed count, the expected proportion, the expected count (n × proportion), the standardized residual and the contribution to χ²:

χ² = Σ (O − E)² / E, df = k − 1, residual = (O − E) / √(n p (1 − p))

A residual greater than 2 in absolute value flags the category that deviates most from what was expected; when the test rejects, the conclusion names it. If any category expects fewer than five observations, the chi-square approximation may not be good, and the app warns about it: it is better to merge categories or to use an exact test.

i“Consistent” is not “proven”

A high p value says that the data do not contradict the expected ratio, not that they prove it. With few observations, the test has no power to reject almost anything. Mendel’s 556 seeds are enough to tell 9:3:3:1 apart from very different ratios, not from similar ones.

4.7Model assumptions

ΣWhy Brown-Forsythe and not Bartlett

Bartlett’s test is very sensitive to non-normality: with heavy-tailed residuals it rejects equality of variances even when the variances are equal. The Brown-Forsythe test (Levene’s test with the median instead of the mean) is robust to this, which is why it decides the row; Bartlett stays in the table as a reference. For a similar reason, with very large data sets you should look at the charts as well as the p-values: with thousands of residuals, tiny departures from normality already turn out significant (hence the normality exception with 50 residuals or more).

With treatments A and B of the poisons, the variances differ greatly (F = 0.102, p < 0.001). iA t test is not for more than two treatments

With three or more treatments, comparing them two at a time with t tests inflates the probability of finding differences that do not exist. The way to go is the analysis of variance of the design (section 4.10) followed by a mean comparison that controls that error (section 4.11).

It also asks for the Blocks column, with one value per treatment in each block. The pairwise comparisons can be Conover, Nemenyi or paired Wilcoxon, with the same adjustments; Tukey (single-step) only with Conover. Besides χ² and its p, it gives Kendall’s W, the agreement among blocks (from 0, none, to 1, all blocks rank the treatments the same way), and the table of treatments sorted by their rank sum, with their letters.

The data must form a complete table of blocks by treatments:

  • A block with an empty response in some treatment is left out, as in R, and a note says how many. With the avocado thrips, two of the six blocks have a plot with no value: the test uses the other four (χ² = 8.308, df = 4, p = 0.081; W = 0.519) and the chart shows only those blocks.
  • If the row of some block and treatment combination is missing, the app stops: “Friedman needs the same treatments in all blocks, and some combinations are missing”.
  • If a treatment has more than one value in a block (replicates, or a factorial in blocks), it also stops, and proposes averaging the replicates of each treatment in each block in the spreadsheet, importing that table of means and trying again. That is what happens with the maize if you ask for the hybrids in blocks: each block has four plots of each hybrid, one per rate.
iWhat you lose with ranks

Rank tests say whether one group tends to have higher values than another, not how much their means differ, and with normal data they have somewhat less power than ANOVA. Before turning to them, it is worth trying the transformation suggested by the assumption check (section 4.7), or a generalized linear model if the response is a count or a proportion (section 4.17).

ΣThe three types of sums of squares

With balanced data (the same number of observations in each combination), the three types give the same result, and the table note says so. With unbalanced data (missing plots, treatments with different numbers of replicates), each type tests a different hypothesis:

  • Type I (sequential): each term adjusted for those that come before it; the order matters.
  • Type II (marginality): each term adjusted for the other terms that do not contain it; it is the most powerful if there is no interaction.
  • Type III (marginal means, the default option): each term adjusted for all the others, with sum-to-zero contrasts; it tests the marginal means with equal weights, which is almost always what you want in an experiment.

What the result includes

  • The conclusion for each effect (“hibrido: there are differences (F = 60.635, p < 0.001)”) and the trial indicators: grand mean, coefficient of variation (one per error in designs with strata: CV(a), CV(b)…) and, with a single error, R².
  • The chart: with one factor, boxes with the points of each treatment and its mean; with two or more, the interaction plot of the first two, one line per level of the second (figure 4.7). Above it is the Chart theme selector (section 4.5).
  • The Compare the means of this design button, which opens the mean comparison (section 4.11) with the same design and the same variables.
  • The tables: the analysis of variance; the trial summary (grand mean, observations, CV, R² and the relative efficiency, where it applies); the means of each factor and of the combinations of the first two; and, with two or more factors in a single-error design, the simple effects: the F test of each factor within each level of the other. With a single error the means are the estimated marginal means, with their standard error (a mean that cannot be estimated appears as “—”); in designs with strata they are the averages of each level, with their number of values.
  • At the bottom of the eight designs (not of the general linear model), the Continue in the LABG suite band suggests AgriDesign, which generates the randomization, the field layout and the field sheet of the design before sowing; with data loaded, its Send data to AgriDesign button downloads the table and opens the app (chapter 10).

With two or more factors, if the interaction is significant the conclusion warns about it. In single-error designs it refers you to the simple effects and to the means of the combinations; in designs with strata, which do not have the simple-effects table, it says to compare one factor within each level of the other in Mean comparisons, with Within each level of (section 4.11).

Relative efficiency

The trial summary says how much the trial gained by blocking (Gomez and Gomez, 1984), as a percentage and with the degrees-of-freedom adjustment factor when the error has fewer than 20:

  • In randomized complete blocks, relative to completely randomized. In the maize it is 160%: a completely randomized design would have needed 60% more replicates for the same precision. It is not computed with missing plots or with generalized blocks.
  • In the Latin square, three: relative to completely randomized, relative to blocks with the rows as blocks and relative to blocks with the columns as blocks. With the rows as blocks, the variation among columns would go into the error: that is why that efficiency depends on the mean square of the columns (and the other one, on that of the rows). In the wheat, with “hilera” (the planting rows) as the rows: 128.4% relative to completely randomized, 111.1% with the rows as blocks and 123.7% with the columns as blocks; blocking by columns alone would have lost more. It is computed with the complete square (t² plots).

Some situations are recognized automatically and explained in a notice: more than one plot per treatment in each block (generalized blocks: the block × treatment interaction is estimated), missing block and treatment combinations (the means are adjusted), empty cells (the type III sum of squares does not exist and type II is used) or a term confounded with another.

Split plots, split-split plots and strip plots

In these designs each factor was applied to units of different sizes, so each one has its own error: the main-plot factor is tested against error a, and the subplot factor and the interaction against error b (the variation within main plots). Error a is block × main plot when the main plots are in blocks, main plot × replicate if they are completely randomized, and row × column if they are in a Latin square. The table note says which error each term is tested against, and the blocks are not tested because their stratum has no error of its own. Analyzing these trials as an ordinary factorial would use a single error and give false F tests, almost always in favor of the main-plot factor.

Split-split plots add a third stratum (Error(bloque/A/B), with errors a, b and c), and strip plots have three errors: a for the horizontal strip factor (block × A), b for the vertical one (block × B) and c for their interaction. With the maize laid out in strips, for example, errors a, b and c have 6, 9 and 18 degrees of freedom. In split-split plots, the table of means of the combinations and the interaction plot show only the first two factors (A × B); the other interactions are studied in Mean comparisons.

The example Rice: irrigation × variety × nitrogen (split-split plot), fictitious and for practice, shows this (figure below): irrigation, in the main plots, is tested with only 2 degrees of freedom of error a (F = 95.839, p = 0.010); the irrigation × nitrogen interaction is significant (F = 23.273, p < 0.001), and the conclusion asks you to compare one factor within each level of the other; the chart and the table of combinations, on the other hand, show irrigation × variety. The coefficients of variation decrease from error a to error c: 8.5, 3.1 and 2.2%. For split plots there is another practice example, Wheat: irrigation × variety (split plot).

With unbalanced data (missing or extra plots), the F tests of the strata are no longer exact and the app warns about it; in that case the mixed model of section 4.15, which handles the imbalance, is the better choice. If an error term is confounded with another, a notice says that the error model is singular.

Incomplete blocks: BIB, lattice and alpha-lattice

When there are more treatments than plots fit in a homogeneous block, each block receives only some of them. The intra-block analysis compares the treatments within the blocks: their sum of squares is sequential (type I), with the treatments last, adjusted for the blocks, and their means are adjusted means. The table note recalls this. The F of the blocks, on the other hand, is not adjusted for the treatments and is only an indication.

In balanced incomplete blocks, the trial summary also includes the efficiency factor E = λt/(rk) and λ, the number of times each pair of treatments occurs together in a block. With the example Sorghum: seven lines in incomplete blocks (BIB), fictitious and for practice (7 lines in 7 blocks of 3), the lines differ (F = 8.604, p = 0.004), with E = 0.778 and λ = 1. If the design is not an exact BIB (blocks of different sizes or pairs that do not occur together the same number of times), the app says so and does not compute the efficiency; the intra-block analysis remains valid.

In these two designs, the box plot shows the raw data of each treatment, not adjusted for the blocks; the means you should publish are the adjusted ones in the table, with their standard error. The intra-block analysis wastes the information there is between blocks. To recover it, with blocks as a random effect, there is Lattice and alpha-lattice (inter-block recovery), among the mixed models (section 4.15); for the heritability of the genotypes, see section 4.16.

The general linear model

For models that are not a design in the catalog, General linear model (formula) asks for the Response variable and the Model terms (what goes after “~”), with the notation of section 4.4; below, the app explains how it read each term. It also asks for the Numeric columns that are treatments (factor), the Sums of squares, the Confidence level and the Significance level. It does not accept error strata (Error(…): that is what the split-plot designs are for) or functions inside the formula (the transformation is done in the Data block); without an intercept, it uses type I sums of squares.

It delivers the analysis of variance, the coefficients (with treatment contrasts: each level compared with the first), a chart of the marginal means (of the first interaction between two factors or, if there is none, of the factor with the largest F; with no factors, observed vs. fitted values) and the means of each factor with their confidence interval. It does not include the trial summary, the efficiency or the suite band. It is also what the model builder uses when the formula is written by hand.

4.11Mean comparisons and contrasts

An analysis of variance tells you whether the treatments differ, not which ones. That is what the four methods of this family are for; they work on the model of any of the eight designs in the previous section: the form starts with the Experimental design and its roles, and then asks what to compare.

MethodWhat for

The second is Within each level of, which by default says — all together — and accepts a single factor. If the interaction is significant, comparing the means of one factor averaged over the other can be misleading; you then compare one factor within each level of the other (the rates within each hybrid), and the result includes a conclusion, its tables and a chart for each level (up to six charts).

With the rice of the previous section, for example, Means of nitrogen (“nitrogeno”) Within each level of irrigation (“riego”) shows the interaction: the N80 rate outperforms N0 by 0.731 under “Temporal” (rainfed) and by 1.156 under “Riego” (irrigated) (both with p < 0.001, with the 12 df of error c).

Dunnett

Dunnett’s p-values are exact and single-step: they come from the multivariate t distribution of the comparisons against the control and are computed in a few seconds, also in balanced incomplete blocks. With the avocado thrips (complete blocks with two missing plots), the hypothesis Less than control finds that only Spinosad reduces thrips relative to the control, “Testigo” (−6.333 thrips per shoot, p = 0.016).

iWhen a test does not run
  • Dunnett needs a single error. In split plots, split-split plots or strip plots, the app stops and explains that Bonferroni and Holm, which adjust all pairs, can be used instead, although against the control they are conservative. It also stops when the comparisons against the control do not have the correlation structure that the exact calculation requires, as in the alpha-lattice of 24 genotypes or with certain missing plots: the message proposes the same.
  • Duncan and Student-Newman-Keuls also need a single error; in split plots, the app proposes Tukey, LSD or Bonferroni.
  • Games-Howell is only for one factor in a completely randomized design, without Within each level of; in other designs, the app proposes Tukey or Bonferroni.
  • Adjusted means needs a single error; in split plots, split-split plots or strip plots, the means are compared with Mean comparisons.

Contrasts

A contrast compares groups of treatments with coefficients that add up to zero. They are written in the Contrasts (one per line) field, in the form nombre: coeficientes (name: coefficients), in the order of the levels that the help shows below it (the coefficients are separated by spaces, commas or semicolons; a line without a name is called “Contrast 1”, “Contrast 2”…). With the field empty, the app shows the first level against the rest as an example:

Each contrast includes its estimate, SE, df, t, p and, in single-error designs, its sum of squares. If the contrasts are orthogonal to each other (with the same number of replicates per level, their coefficients multiplied term by term add up to zero; the app checks this with the actual replicates of each level), their sums of squares partition that of the treatments without overlapping, and the note says so; if not, it warns that they overlap. A line that does not add up to zero is also computed, with the note that it is a linear combination and not a contrast. Contrasts are planned before looking at the data; chosen afterwards, they have the problem of multiple comparisons.

Orthogonal polynomials

With a quantitative factor (nitrogen rates, plant densities, temperatures), what matters is not the differences between rates but the shape of the response. Orthogonal polynomials split the variation among rates into a linear component, a quadratic one, a cubic one… (as many as the number of rates minus one), each with its own test. You choose the Quantitative factor (rate), whose levels must be numbers (at least three), and the Curve degree (Straight line, Quadratic (with the optimum rate) or Cubic), which decides the fitted curve; the table always includes all the components.

The orthogonal coefficients are computed on the actual levels of the rate, even if they are not equally spaced. With the quadratic curve, the app fits the equation to the means (y = 4.454 + 0.03133·x − 1.060·10−4·x² in the maize, with R² = 0.998) and gives the rate at the maximum or minimum; if it falls outside the rates tested, it warns about it, because extrapolating is not advisable. With Within each level of there is one curve per level: in the maize, the maximum of each hybrid is at 147.5 (H-40), 139.5 (H-52) and 157.7 kg/ha (H-66). For a response that levels off at a plateau, the linear-plateau model (section 4.18) is the better choice.

Adjusted means

The estimated marginal means are the averages that the model predicts for each level, with equal weights over the other factors. With balanced data they are the usual means; with missing plots, incomplete blocks or covariates, they are the ones you should publish. For the chosen factor (and within each level of another, if requested), the method gives the mean, its SE, the degrees of freedom and its confidence interval at the chosen Confidence level, with a chart of the means and their intervals. In the alpha-lattice, for example, the 24 adjusted means have standard errors from 0.194 to 0.195, with 31 df.

4.12Analysis of covariance and regression

When the response depends on a numeric variable measured on each plot (the number of plants, the altitude of the site, the rate), the question changes: it is no longer enough to know whether the treatments differ; you want to know how much the response changes per unit of that variable. In the catalog, the ANCOVA and regression family has five methods.

Analysis of covariance

The form asks for the Response variable, the Treatment factors (up to 2, crossed), the Blocks (with — no blocks (completely randomized) — if the trial has none) and the Covariates (up to 2). The options are the Sums of squares (Type III (marginal means) by default, Type II (marginality) or Type I (treatments adjusted for the covariate)), the Comparison of adjusted means (Tukey, Fisher’s LSD, Bonferroni, Šidák, Holm, Scheffé or None), the Confidence level and the Significance level. With empty cells, the type III sum of squares does not exist: type II is used, and the note under the table says so.

The sorghum example compares six varieties (five lines and a control) in four blocks; the harvested plants were counted in each plot, and the count was not the same in all of them. A plot with more plants yields more, regardless of the variety: the covariate separates the two things.

ΣThe two assumptions of covariance analysis

The traffic light of the result checks the two assumptions specific to ANCOVA:

  • Equal slopes. The covariate must weigh the same in all treatments: the model with a common slope is compared against one with a slope per treatment (in the sorghum, F = 0.649 with 5 and 9 df, p = 0.670). If the slopes differ, the effect of the variety depends on the number of plants, and a single adjusted mean does not summarize it.
  • The covariate does not depend on the treatments. The covariate is analyzed with the same design (F = 1.391 with 5 and 15 df, p = 0.283). If the treatments changed it (a variety that emerges poorly), adjusting for it would take away part of the variety’s real effect.

The traffic light does not test the normality or the constant variance of the residuals, and Check the model assumptions (section 4.7) does not accept covariates. As an approximation, check the residuals of the same design without the covariate there, and look at the plot to see that the points of each treatment follow their line with an even spread; if the relationship with the covariate is curved, transform the covariate in the Data block (section 2.9).

The adjusted means are the values each treatment would have if all its plots had the same number of plants (the overall mean of the covariate). They are the ones you publish, and they can change the order of the unadjusted means:

Simple and multiple linear regression

Choose the Response variable (y) and the Predictors (up to 15); with one predictor it is simple regression, and with more, multiple regression. The predictors enter the model in the order in which they appear in the list, which is the order of the columns in the table, not the order in which you checked them. The Predict at (optional) field takes one line per point, with the values of the predictors in that same order, separated by spaces; the help under the field names them in order. The Confidence level and the Significance level complete the form. In the coffee example, the yield of 40 farms is explained by their altitude, the soil organic matter and the percentage of shade.

The conclusion says whether the model is significant as a whole, which predictors have an effect of their own (adjusted for the others) and how much it explains (R² and adjusted R²). In simple regression, it says instead how much the response rises or falls per unit of the predictor, with its interval. The regression traffic light has six rows (if one fails, Try the alternative offers the corresponding remedy: section 4.7):

  • Normality of residuals: Shapiro-Wilk.
  • Constant variance: studentized Breusch-Pagan (Koenker).
  • Model form: the RESET test (with ŷ² and ŷ³), and lack of fit when the predictors have repeated values. In the coffee, RESET gives F = 3.576 and p = 0.039: perhaps a curve or an interaction is missing.
  • Independence: Durbin-Watson, with an exact p if there are fewer than 100 observations. It measures autocorrelation in the order of the rows, so it only matters if that is the order of measurement or the position in the field; that is why this row never goes beyond amber.
  • Influential observations: R’s criteria (leverage, DFFITS, DFBETAS, COVRATIO and Cook’s distance). The row turns red if any observation has a large Cook’s distance, and amber if only the other criteria flag it. In the coffee, two farms, 17 and 6, are flagged by COVRATIO and none by Cook’s distance.
  • Collinearity, with two or more predictors: the VIF of each predictor and the condition number; the row turns amber with a VIF greater than 5 and red with one greater than 10 (maximum VIF 1.10 and condition number 15.1 in the coffee: no problem).

With a single predictor, the first plot is not observed against fitted but the fitted line over the data, with the confidence band of the mean (shaded) and the prediction band (dashed border); the other three are the same.

It fits a curve to a single x variable, with the Polynomial degree you choose (2 (quadratic), 3 (cubic) or 4 (quartic)), and tests each degree: the last one that enters must be significant for the curve to need it. With the data of each maize plot, the quadratic curve of yield against rate explains 81.7% of the variation (F = 100.377 with 2 and 45 df) and has its maximum at 147.747 kg/ha, with an estimated 6.769 t/ha: the same maximum given by the orthogonal polynomials on the means (section 4.11), because the data are balanced. With degree 3, the conclusion says that the cubic term is not significant (p = 0.529) and that one degree less is enough. The maximum or minimum is sought in curves of degree 2 and 3 when their last term is significant; if it falls outside the range of the data, the conclusion says so and asks you not to extrapolate it.

Because the rate is repeated (four rates, twelve plots each), the app splits the error into lack of fit and pure error: the lack of fit is not significant (F = 0.402, p = 0.529), so the parabola describes the means of the four rates well. The first plot shows the curve with its confidence band and its prediction band, and the Predict at (optional) field takes x values separated by spaces. iWhen Durbin-Watson lights up for no reason

In the maize, the traffic light of the polynomial regression marks independence in amber (Durbin-Watson = 0.982, p < 0.001). It is not a flaw of the trial: the rows of the example are sorted by block, hybrid and rate, and the residuals of the same block and hybrid resemble each other because the model does not include the hybrid. The test only makes sense if the order of the rows is the order of measurement or the position in the field; that is why the app warns about it in the row itself.

The Criterion can be AIC (like R's step), the default, which at each step makes the change that lowers the AIC the most, or Partial F p-value, with a Level to enter (0.05 by default) and a Level to remove (0.10). iThe AIC of the selection is not that of the regression

The selection uses the AIC of R’s step function, n·ln(SSE/n) + 2k; the goodness-of-fit table of linear regression gives the one derived from the log-likelihood, like R’s AIC(). They differ by a constant that depends only on n: the six-predictor model chosen by the selection has AIC = 52.735 there and 168.250 in linear regression. Compare AIC values only within the same analysis.

4.13Correlation

Correlation measures how much two variables move together, without saying which depends on which. The family includes the correlation between two variables, the correlation matrix of many variables and partial correlation, which discounts the effect of others.

Correlation between two variables

Choose the x variable, the y variable, the Coefficient (Pearson (linear relationship), the default; Spearman (ranks) or Kendall (ranks), for a relationship that only rises or only falls) and the Alternative hypothesis (Correlation different from zero, Positive correlation or Negative correlation). With Pearson, you also choose the Confidence level of the interval; with Spearman or Kendall, the p-value (Exact if possible or Approximate) and the Continuity correction (in the approximate p) checkbox.

In the coffee, altitude and yield have a Pearson correlation r = 0.565 (95% CI: 0.307 to 0.745; t = 4.217 with 38 df, p < 0.001): they share 31.9% of their variation (r² = 0.319). The conclusion rates it as strong using Cohen’s (1988) cutoffs: less than 0.1, negligible; up to 0.3, weak; up to 0.5, moderate; beyond that, strong.

The result includes the scatter plot with the least-squares line and two tables: the test (the coefficient, its interval with Pearson, the statistic, the p-value, how the p was calculated and the number of pairs) and the three coefficients at once (Pearson 0.565, Spearman 0.574 and Kendall 0.406 in the coffee). If Pearson and Spearman differ a lot, the relationship is not linear or some values weigh too much. Without ties, the p-values of Spearman (up to 1,290 observations) and Kendall (fewer than 50) are exact, as in R. With ties, as in the coffee, the p is approximate and the conclusion says so: Spearman calculates it with Student’s t, and Kendall with the normal approximation corrected for ties (z = 3.683).

This analysis has no traffic light: the shape of the relationship and the extreme values can be seen in the plot, and linear regression gives its full diagnostics.

Correlation matrix

With the Variables (2 to 30) you check, it gives all the pairwise correlations with the Coefficient you choose (Pearson, Spearman or Kendall). Because there are many tests at once, the p-values are adjusted (p-value adjustment: Holm, the default, Bonferroni, Benjamini-Hochberg (FDR) or No adjustment). In Rows with missing values, you choose between Only rows complete in all variables (the default) and Each pair with its complete rows.

The conclusion says how many pairs have a significant correlation (in the coffee, 4 of 10) and which one is the strongest.

The first table is the matrix, with stars according to the adjusted p (* p < 0.05; ** p < 0.01; *** p < 0.001); the second gives each pair with its p, its adjusted p and its number of pairs, and with Pearson also its confidence interval. The tests are two-sided. In the coffee, altitude and temperature lie almost on a straight line (r = −0.967): that is why stepwise selection does not want both.

4.14Contingency tables

When both variables are categories (germinated or not, healthy or diseased, treated or control), the data are summarized in a table of counts. The four methods of the family accept two forms of data: one row per case (each seed, each leaf), with — each row is one case — in Frequencies, or the table already counted, with a column of counts chosen in that same field.

The Frequencies field only offers numeric columns of non-negative integers, and it proposes on its own the one named like a count (n, frecuencia, conteo, casos, semillas…). The categorical variables must have 2 to 50 categories. Contingency tables have neither a traffic light nor a suite strip.

Contingency table

It tests whether two variables are independent. Choose the Row variable, the Column variable, the Frequencies, the Yates correction (2 × 2 tables) checkbox (checked by default, as in R), the Confidence level and the Significance level. The treated-seed example counts how many seeds germinated with and without fungicide in four lots; here, the lots are pooled.

Treated seed germinates more (χ² = 31.326, p < 0.001). The conclusion says whether there is an association, how strong it is (Cramér’s V: 0.201, weak) and which cell departs most from independence, by its adjusted residual (beyond ±1.96, the cell departs at α = 0.05; here, fungicide with germinated, 5.678). In 2 × 2 tables, it adds the odds ratio (2.589, 95% CI: 1.854 to 3.615: treated seeds have 2.6 times the odds of germinating) and the relative risk of the first column (1.266, 95% CI: 1.165 to 1.376).

The five tables are the observed frequencies; the expected ones with their adjusted residual; the independence tests (Pearson’s chi-square, the likelihood ratio G² and Fisher’s exact test, with its conditional odds ratio in 2 × 2 tables); the measures of association (φ, Cramér’s V, the contingency coefficient, Goodman and Kruskal’s λ and the uncertainty coefficient); and the measures for ordered categories (γ, Kendall’s τ-b, Stuart’s τ-c and Somers’ d). SigmaPro calculates the latter in any table, but they are only meaningful if both variables have a natural order (doses, damage grades).

Fisher’s exact test is always calculated in 2 × 2 tables; in larger ones, only with up to 5,000 cases and if some expected frequency is less than 5 or there are 300 cases or fewer (if it would require examining too many tables, the note says so and recommends merging categories). When some expected frequency is less than 5, the note warns that the chi-square is approximate, and the conclusion decides with Fisher’s exact test.

McNemar and Bowker: paired data

When each case is classified twice (before and after a treatment, or with two methods), the two classifications are not independent and the chi-square does not apply. Choose the First classification (before) and the Second classification (after), with the same categories, the Frequencies and the Continuity correction checkbox (checked by default). With two categories, it is the McNemar test, with its exact binomial version; with more, Bowker’s symmetry test, which asks whether changes in one direction are as frequent as in the other.

When the data come from several lots, sites or years, pooling them can create or hide an association. Choose the Row variable, the Column variable, the Strata (lots, sites, years) and the Frequencies, with the Continuity correction checkbox (checked by default) and the Confidence level. Controlling for lot, treatment and germination remain associated (CMH χ² = 31.600, p < 0.001), with a common odds ratio of 2.621 (95% CI: 1.871 to 3.671). Lot 4 has the highest ratio (6.093), but Breslow-Day finds no differences between lots (p = 0.186), so the common ratio summarizes them well.

The weighted kappa appears when there are more than two categories.

4.15Linear mixed models

A mixed model separates the fixed effects, those chosen on purpose and about which you want to draw conclusions (the hybrids, the rates), from the random effects, which represent a sample of many possible ones (blocks, locations, years, plants measured several times). With it, each source of variation carries its own error, missing plots do not break the analysis, and the information between blocks is not lost.

The six methods of the family run in the R engine inside the browser (section 4.6), with the packages lme4, lmerTest and emmeans; repeated measures use nlme. The engine is downloaded the first time; after that it works offline.

The Model terms (what goes after “~”) field takes the right-hand side of the formula. Fixed effects are written as in the general linear model (section 4.10); each random effect goes in parentheses with its grouping:

Next come Compare the means of (checkboxes with the fixed factors that enter as main effects; by default, the first three), Within each level of (with two or more factors, to compare one separately at each level of another when they interact), the Comparison adjustment (Tukey, Bonferroni, Holm, Šidák or No adjustment (LSD)), the Sums of squares (type III, II or I), the Degrees of freedom for the tests (Satterthwaite, or Kenward-Roger, more exact with few groups and slower) and the Estimation: REML, which gives unbiased variance components, or maximum likelihood, to compare models with different fixed effects. With the maize and random blocks (hibrido * dosis + (1 | bloque)), the tests of the hybrids, the rates and their interaction give the same F values as the block ANOVA (60.63, 292.15 and 1.47), because the data are balanced. What changes is the standard error of the means: it includes the block variance (43.9% of the random variation), so the hybrid means carry an SE of 0.1079 with 4.25 degrees of freedom, while their differences, in which the blocks cancel out, keep the 33 degrees of freedom of the error.

With the two maize factors, the result brings ten tables: the tests of the fixed effects, the variance components with their percentage, the test of the random effects (likelihood ratio, like lmerTest::ranova; its p is conservative, because a variance cannot be negative, and the true one is usually half of it), the marginal means of each factor with their letters and their pairwise comparisons, the coefficients, the predictions of the random effects (BLUP) and the model fit. When a factor has more than 45 pairs, its table of pairs is not shown, though the letters are; with more than 50 levels, only the means. If the BLUPs exceed 120 rows, their table does not appear either: a note below the coefficients says so, and the equivalent R code gives them with ranef().

The plots are the marginal means of the first compared factor, with their interval and their letters (with Within each level of, one line for each level of the other factor), the residuals against the fitted values and their Q-Q plot. The traffic light checks the normality of the residuals (Shapiro-Wilk), the normality of the random effects (the BLUPs of the first grouping, if it has 5 levels or more), the equality of variances (Levene with the median, among the levels of the first fixed factor) and the optimizer warnings; a singular fit shows in amber.

4.16Variance components, heritability and repeatability

Three methods for quantitative genetics and breeding, which run in the browser without the R engine: they estimate the variance components by REML (with the average information algorithm) and were validated against lme4.

Variance components

They tell how much variation each random factor contributes. Choose the Random factors (up to 4), How they are related (Crossed; Nested (each within the previous ones), in table order; or Crossed, with their two-way interactions) and, if needed, the Fixed factors (optional, up to 3), which enter as main effects and are not tested: for their F tests there is the linear mixed model. In the alpha-lattice, with the genotypes fixed and the blocks nested in the replicates, the replicate contributes 43.6% of the variance (σ² = 0.1139), the blocks within the replicate 23.7% and the error 32.6%. Each component carries its SE and its likelihood ratio test, with the p of the mixture ½χ²0 + ½χ²1, because a variance cannot be negative: the replicate gives χ² = 6.41 (p = 0.006) and the blocks, χ² = 5.01 (p = 0.013). With only three replicates, the replicate variance is very imprecise: its SE, 0.128, is larger than the estimate. The plot shows the percentage of the total variance that each component contributes.

iTwo p values for the same variance

For a random intercept, the test of the mixed models of section 4.15 (ranova) gives the p of χ² with 1 degree of freedom, which is conservative; these three methods give that of the mixture ½χ²0 + ½χ²1, which is half as large. With the same model (and REML), the variance and the χ² agree: only the way the p is calculated changes.

Heritability and genotype BLUPs

With the genotypes as random, the method estimates the genetic variance, the heritability and the genotypic value of each one. Choose the Genotypes (random), the Replicate or complete block (fixed), the Incomplete block within replicate (random), for the alpha-lattice and other designs with incomplete blocks, and the Environment or location (fixed); with several environments, the genotype × environment interaction enters as random and the conclusion compares its variance with the genetic variance. Below the roles, the app shows the model it built (“Model: …”), and the title of the result states the design it understood. For the selection gain, choose the Selected proportion (from 5% to 50%) and, in Select the values that are, the highest (yield, weight, content) or the lowest (days to flowering, height, damage).

!Specify all the columns of the design

If the replicate or the incomplete block is missing, its variation goes into the error, without warning, and the heritability usually drops: with the alpha-lattice, the mean-basis H² is 0.840 with the replicates and the blocks, 0.780 with the replicates only and 0.580 with neither (the title then says “completely randomized”). The conclusion begins with the genetic variance (σ²g = 0.1429, χ² = 23.62, p < 0.001). The mean-basis heritability is 0.840 ± 0.063: 84% of the variation among the genotype means is genetic. 4.17Generalized linear models

Counts (insects per shoot, lesions per leaf), proportions (seeds germinated out of those sown) and times or concentrations do not have the constant variance or the normal distribution that ANOVA requires. Instead of transforming them, a generalized linear model uses the distribution that suits them and a link that connects the mean with the treatments, and it presents the results on the original scale: rates, probabilities and means.

MethodPurpose
Counts (Poisson and negative binomial)Poisson, quasi-Poisson or negative binomial, chosen by the overdispersion; treatments in blocks, covariates and exposure.
Proportions (binomial)Binomial or quasibinomial: successes out of n trials or 0/1 responses; logit, probit or complementary log-log link; Firth correction for separation.

Counts

Choose Response: the count, the Treatment factors (up to 3, crossed), the Blocks, the Covariates (optional, up to 2) and the Exposure (optional: area or time sampled), when the units were not sampled equally: with it, the means are rates per unit. The default Family is Automatic (based on overdispersion): the Poisson is fitted and, if Pearson’s test finds overdispersion (more variation than the Poisson allows: p < 0.05 and χ²/df greater than 1.1), the analysis switches to the negative binomial. You can also set the Poisson, the quasi-Poisson or the negative binomial.

The Link is the log; the Poisson and the quasi-Poisson also allow the identity, the square root and the power (μλ, with its Link exponent λ (μ^λ)), and the negative binomial, the identity and the square root. With the automatic family, the list offers only the links that the negative binomial also has (log, identity and square root), because the analysis switches to it if there is overdispersion. Next come the Analysis of deviance (type II by default, type III with sum-to-zero contrasts or sequential type I), the Coefficient intervals (see below) and, as in the mixed models, Compare the means of, Within each level of and the Comparison adjustment.

With the avocado thrips, the Poisson had overdispersion (χ²/df = 2.23, p = 0.002) and the app switched to the negative binomial (θ = 8.390). The block has no effect (p = 0.468) and the treatment does (χ² = 21.34, 4 df, p < 0.001): spinosad leaves 1.483 thrips per shoot, against 7.761 in the control.

The rates are compared on their own scale, with asymmetric intervals (calculated on the link scale and transformed back) and Tukey letters. The tables are the analysis of deviance, the coefficients on the link scale, the rate of each treatment, their pairwise comparisons (as rate ratios) and the model fit. Besides the plot of the rates, the result includes the plot of the Pearson residuals against the fitted values. The traffic light checks overdispersion, Pearson residuals greater than 3 and convergence.

Proportions and skewed responses

For proportions, the Response: successes (germinated, diseased) or 0/1 holds the successes, and you choose the column of Trials in each row (seeds sown, plants assessed), or — the response is 0/1 —. The automatic family starts with the binomial and switches to the quasibinomial if there is overdispersion (with 0/1 it stays with the binomial); the link is logit, probit or complementary log-log. With the beans, the binomial is enough (Pearson χ²/df = 1.18, p = 0.281): the treatment changes germination (χ² = 241.95, 4 df), from 0.2800 in the control to 0.9450 with scarification.

Skewed positive responses are analyzed with the gamma, for times and concentrations whose variance grows with the square of the mean, or with the inverse Gaussian, more skewed, whose variance grows with the cube. The method uses the log by default; the gamma also allows the inverse, the identity and the power, and the inverse Gaussian, 1/μ² (its canonical link), the inverse, the identity and the power. With Box and Cox’s (1964) poisons and the gamma, poison (F = 48.92) and treatment (F = 23.68) have an effect and their interaction does not (p = 0.192), without transforming the survival time as in section 2.9. With the free-dispersion families (gamma, inverse Gaussian, quasi-Poisson and quasibinomial), the dispersion is estimated by Pearson and the tests are F tests; the mean comparisons use t in the gamma and z in the others, like emmeans.

Profile intervals and Firth correction

With the log and logit links, each coefficient carries its ratio (rate ratio, odds ratio or ratio of means) with its interval. In Coefficient intervals, choose Wald (estimate ± z·SE), symmetric on the link scale, or Profile likelihood, like R’s confint() but with the exact root of the profile: it can be asymmetric and is more exact with few data or probabilities near 0 or 1. With the thrips, the rate ratio of spinosad against the control is 0.191; its 95% interval goes from 0.085 to 0.430 with Wald and from 0.080 to 0.418 with the profile. With other links, the profile interval is given on the link scale. In the free-dispersion families the profile uses the Pearson dispersion, and in the negative binomial it keeps θ fixed; if the likelihood does not drop enough toward one side, that limit is shown as “—” and a warning says so.

With 0/1 responses or few trials, separation can occur: a group with 0% or 100% successes. Maximum likelihood drives its coefficient to infinity, and its SE is no longer useful. The Firth correction (for separation: groups with 0% or 100%) checkbox, which appears with the binomial family (or the automatic one) and the logit link, penalizes the likelihood: it gives finite coefficients, tests each coefficient and each term with the penalized likelihood ratio and gives the intervals from the penalized profile, like the logistf package; there is no AIC. When the ordinary fit has separation, its warning ends with “Use Firth’s correction.” and the fit row of the traffic light shows in red.

With the example “Bean: days to germination” (120 seeds from three treatments and three lots), the response germino (0/1) and the formula lote + tratamiento + peso_semilla, all 40 scarified seeds germinated. The generalized linear model with a formula accepts in Model terms (what goes after “~”) the same terms as the general linear model, with the Numeric columns that are factors (rate, numbered block) checkboxes, and the seven families: binomial, quasibinomial, Poisson, quasi-Poisson, negative binomial, gamma and inverse Gaussian. Generalized mixed models run in the R engine (lme4, car and emmeans). Counts or proportions in random blocks (mixed) asks for the Treatment factors (up to 3, crossed) and the Blocks (random); Generalized linear mixed model (formula) asks for the Model terms including the random effects (what goes after “~”), such as tratamiento + (1 | bloque) or (1 | localidad/bloque). Both accept the trials column (with the binomial) and the exposure (with the Poisson or the negative binomial). The families are Poisson, binomial, negative binomial and gamma; the gamma allows only random intercepts, such as (1 | bloque), and estimates its dispersion. The model builder (section 4.4) sends a count response (Poisson) or a positive skewed response (gamma) with random effects to the formula model.

  • Likelihood approximation: Laplace (1 node), by default, or adaptive Gauss-Hermite quadrature with 5, 10 or 25 nodes, more exact, especially with proportions from few trials. It appears with the binomial or the Poisson and a single random effect, an intercept per group (in the random-blocks method, always). The observation-level effect is a second random effect: with Yes, always, the app asks you to use Laplace; if the automatic mode adds it, the app returns to Laplace on its own and says so.
  • Test each random effect (likelihood ratio), checked by default: compares the model with the one without that effect (with a model without random effects if none is left); its p is conservative. With the gamma it is not offered, because its Laplace likelihood cannot be compared with that of the model without random effects.
  • Observation-level effect (overdispersion), with the Poisson or the binomial with trials: Automatic: if the Pearson test calls for it (p < 0.05 and χ²/df greater than 1.1), Yes, always or No. It adds a random effect per row that absorbs the extra variation (Harrison, 2014).
  • Fixed-effect tests: Type II Wald χ² or Type III Wald χ² (sum-to-zero).

The result includes the Wald tests (their note states the approximation used), the coefficients (with the log and logit links, also their ratios with Wald intervals), the variances of the random effects on the link scale, the test of each random effect, the means on the response scale (with the random effects at zero), their pairwise comparisons and the fit, with the θ of the negative binomial or the dispersion of the gamma. The traffic light checks overdispersion (or dispersion), Pearson residuals greater than 3 and the optimizer warnings.

4.18Nonlinear models

Growth that slows down as it approaches its final size, a herbicide response that falls in an S shape, or a yield that stops rising with more fertilizer are neither straight lines nor parabolas: they have parameters with biological meaning (the asymptote, the dose for 50% effect, the optimum rate) that only a nonlinear model estimates directly. The family includes three methods.

Growth curves

Three models: the logistic (symmetric around its inflection point), the Gompertz (which slows down more gradually) and the Weibull (the most flexible). With maize height on five dates and one logistic curve per nitrogen rate, the model explains 99.1% of the variation and the curves differ (F = 12.40 with 9 and 68 df). With Lowest AIC (fits all and compares) the logistic also wins: the Weibull, with one more parameter per curve, is at ΔAIC = 3.73, and the Gompertz, at 41.22.

The comparison by parameter tells what changed: the asymptote (the final height) differs between rates (F = 9.46, p < 0.001), from 229.3 without nitrogen (N0) to 268.0 with N180; the inflection point (around 39 days in all four) and the scale do not differ (p = 0.414 and 0.895).

The parameter table gives each estimate with its SE, its interval and its meaning, and the residual plot completes the diagnosis.

Dose-response

The 4-parameter log-logistic model (upper limit d and lower limit c, slope b and the ED50 e) is the standard for herbicides, fungicides and toxicants (Seefeldt et al., 1995); the 3-parameter model fixes the lower limit at zero. The derived values are the doses for 10, 50 and 90% effect, with their SE by the delta method.

The ED50 of glyphosate is 75.13 (95% CI: 63.32 to 86.94) and that of glufosinate 174.2 (121.6 to 226.7): it takes 2.3 times more glufosinate for the same effect. The curves differ (F = 52.14), but only in the ED50 (F = 29.45); the slope and the two limits can be common (p = 0.732, 0.508 and 0.178).

The ED90 of glufosinate (752.1) falls outside the doses tested, and its interval is very wide (237.9 to 1266): extrapolating the curve costs precision.

The Brain-Cousens models (the 5-parameter one, and the 4-parameter one with the lower limit at zero) add a parameter f for hormesis: a stimulus at low doses before the decline, frequent with herbicides at sublethal doses. With f > 0 and b > 1, the derived values are the dose of maximum response, the maximum response and the ED50, which here is the dose, after the maximum, at which the response falls halfway between d and c; their SEs come from the implicit derivative, like ED and MAX in drc. In these models the parameter e is no longer the ED50, and the meaning column says so. If there is no stimulus, the conclusion points it out: in the herbicides, f comes out negative (−0.027 for glyphosate and −0.017 for glufosinate), there is no dose of maximum response, and a log-logistic is enough.

With it, the ED50s are 79.41 (95% CI: 70.51 to 88.32) and 166.6 (150.4 to 182.8).

With Profile likelihood, the interval of the parameter e for glufosinate (its ED50 in the log-logistic) goes from 121.6 to 226.7 (Wald) to 137.2 to 294.2: asymmetric, longer toward the high doses, where there is less information. The table of derived values keeps Wald intervals by the delta method, which are symmetric and, with poorly bounded curves, can reach negative values.

4.19Response surfaces

When two or more quantitative factors act together (nitrogen and potassium, temperature and time), response surface methodology looks for the combination that gives the best response. The factors (numeric, from 2 to 6) are coded (center at 0 and a step of 1) so that the coefficients are comparable, and the app converts everything back to the original units. The family includes five methods.

What matters then is in the conclusion, which searches along the ridge: the lowest estimated response is 45.66, with N = 102.1 and K = 112.4.

First order and steepest ascent

Far from the optimum, a factorial with center points and a first-order model are enough: the path of steepest ascent tells you which way to move. With the tomato, the flat model has lack of fit (F = 11.32, p = 0.017): the response curves, and the app advises moving to the second order. The path rises 51.53 kg of N and 25.62 of K per coded unit; its table gives the combinations and the predicted response.

Mixtures

In a mixture, the proportions matter, not the amounts: if one component goes up, another goes down. Scheffé models have no intercept and are fitted on the proportions: each row is divided by its sum, so proportions, percentages or amounts all work, as long as every row adds up to the same total (if not, the app warns about it). Choose the response, the Mixture components (2 to 6; each row sums to the same total), the Scheffé model (Linear (each component alone), Quadratic (with binary blends) or Special cubic (with ternary blends)) and, in Find, Mixture with the highest response or Mixture with the lowest response.

With Cornell’s (2002) yarn elongation, a simplex-lattice of polyethylene, polystyrene and polypropylene with replicates, the quadratic model explains 95.1% of the variation. The coefficient of each component is the response of the pure component (11.7, 9.4 and 16.4), and that of each pair, how far the blend departs from the sum of its parts: polyethylene with polystyrene (β = 19.0, p < 0.001) and with polypropylene (β = 11.4, p = 0.002) are synergies; polystyrene with polypropylene, an antagonism (β = −9.6, p = 0.005).

The best mixture of the model, searched over the whole triangle, has 29.4% polyethylene and 70.6% polypropylene, without polystyrene: an estimated elongation of 17.38 (95% CI: 16.36 to 18.41), more than any pure component.

The ANOVA tests the model against the mean (the proportions add up to 1, so the mean is already in the model) and the lack of fit against the pure error of the replicated mixtures. In the yarn, the quadratic has as many coefficients as distinct mixtures (6), and that row of the traffic light stays gray: there is nothing to test it with. The linear model does show lack of fit (F = 32.32 with 3 and 9 df, p < 0.001) and explains only 42.7%. The special cubic cannot be estimated with this design: the app says “The mixtures do not allow the special cubic model to be estimated” and explains that it needs the centroid of each three-component face. Besides the triangle (with three components), the Cox trace moves each component from 0 to 1 starting at the centroid and divides the rest equally among the others: the slope of each line tells how much weight that component carries. The method has no residual plot.

Desirability of several responses

A single response is rarely all that matters: you want high yield, low cost and a quality at a certain value. Derringer and Suich’s desirability fits each response with its own second-order model (with the same design and the same coding), converts each prediction into a d between 0 (unacceptable) and 1 (what is sought) and combines them into an overall desirability D, their geometric mean: a single response outside its limits is enough for D to be 0. The combination with the highest D is sought with the Nelder-Mead method from each design point and from the center.

Choose the Design factors (numeric, 2 to 6) and the Responses to combine (1 to 6). Each response has its own box: Find (Highest, Lowest or A target value), the Lower limit and the Upper limit (with the Target value between them, if you look for a value), the Exponent (1: linear; greater than 1 requires getting closer to the target; with a target value, the Exponent (above the target) governs the other side) and the Importance, which weights the response in the geometric mean. When you select a response, it looks for the highest value, with the limits at its minimum and maximum: you have to adjust them to what you require of each one. The Search region is The cube of the design levels or The design sphere (rotatable central composite).

In the fruit example (a central composite of nitrogen and irrigation depth), with the highest yield between 70 and 86, the lowest cost between 11 and 15 and a pH target of 6.5 between 6 and 7, the most desirable combination is N = 175.9 and riego = 25.89, with D = 0.786. There the estimated yield is 79.50 (d = 0.593), the cost 11.72 (d = 0.819) and the pH 6.500 (d = 1.000). With the sphere, the same point comes out, because it lies close to the center.

The tables give the combination (coded and original), each response at that point with its d and the R² of its model (0.995, 0.992 and 0.976), and the D of each run, with the predicted responses: the eight runs on the edge get D = 0, because some response falls outside its limits, and the five at the center, 0.743.

The method has no traffic light: if the model of any response has an R² below 0.7, the conclusion warns that its prediction, and with it the desirability, is not very reliable.

Combined analysis

With the oats of Olivoto and Lúcio (2020), 10 genotypes in 14 environments with three replicates, the combined ANOVA finds the interaction (F = 2.76, p < 0.001): the ranking of the genotypes changes between environments. The genotypes are tested in two ways: against the error (F = 14.93) and against the interaction, as is appropriate if the environments are a sample of many possible ones (F = 5.41, the same as in the mixed model of section 4.15). G8 has the highest overall mean (3.004).

The method also analyzes each environment separately (mean, MSE, CV and the genotype F) and checks the homogeneity of the errors: in the oats, the largest MSE is 6.71 times the smallest (Bartlett p < 0.001), so the tests of the combined analysis are approximate, and the traffic light says so. With 12 genotypes or fewer, the Each genotype across environments plot joins the means of each genotype in the environments ordered from the lowest to the highest mean: the lines that cross are the interaction.

Two-stage analysis

When the errors of the environments differ as much as in the oats, or genotypes are missing in some environments, the two-stage analysis is the better choice. In the first stage, each environment is analyzed on its own and gives the adjusted mean of each genotype with its variance; in the second, those means are combined by generalized least squares, each weighted according to its precision, and the interaction variance is estimated by REML. It does not impute: it uses the means that exist. Each environment needs its own error, so replicates or several plots per genotype are required.

In the oats, the genotypes differ (Wald: F = 5.99 with 9 and 117 df, p < 0.001), G8 has the highest adjusted mean (3.016 ± 0.07732), and the interaction variance, now without the error of each environment, is 0.05407. The traffic light row for the errors shows in amber (maximum/minimum ratio of 6.71): this is exactly the case the method is meant for. The tables are the first stage (mean, MSE, CV and genotypes of each environment), the adjusted means next to the simple mean, and the test of the genotypes; the plot gives each adjusted mean with its 95% interval. When there are replicates, the assistant (section 4.2) includes it among the next steps of any analysis in this family.

Stability

There are several ways to measure it. The regression on the environmental index (Finlay and Wilkinson; Eberhart and Russell) gives each genotype a slope b (1: it responds like the average; greater: it takes advantage of good environments; smaller: it is adapted to poor ones) and a variance of the deviations S²d (0: its response is predictable). Wricke’s ecovalence and Shukla’s variance tell how much each genotype contributes to the interaction, and Lin and Binns’ superiority (Pi) how far it is from the best genotype in each environment. Huehn’s nonparametric statistics work with ranks: S1 and S2 measure how much each genotype changes place between environments (with their Z test), and S3 and S6 combine response and stability. Kang’s YS gives each genotype points for its yield rank, subtracts points for its Shukla variance and selects those above the average; it needs replicates. Annicchiarico’s reliability index (Wi) expresses the response as a % of the mean of each environment and penalizes instability (Wi = mean − z·SD), across all environments, in the favorable ones and in the unfavorable ones; it is calculated only when the best is the highest response.

The options are the Standard error of b, From each genotype's regression or From the pooled error of the environments, and the Risk level of the Annicchiarico index (0.25, 0.10 or 0.05): a lower risk penalizes instability more.

The additive main effects and multiplicative interaction model decomposes the interaction into axes (principal components of the table of interactions). The app tests each axis with Gollob’s F and, with Cornelius’s FR, the interaction that remains after n axes, which is more reliable: in the oats, Cornelius retains 3 axes and Gollob 4; the first two explain 66.22% of the interaction. iTwo ways of counting the axes

The AMMI conclusion counts the axes that are significant by Gollob consecutively from the first one; the WAAS counts all the axes with a p below the significance level and uses that number of axes, starting from the first (without replicates there is no error to test them, and it uses all of them). If a non-significant axis fell between two significant ones, the two numbers would differ; in the oats they agree (4). To set another number, choose the axes in WAAS axes.

4.21Multivariate analysis

When several variables are measured at once on each unit (the length, width, weight and wall of a fruit), analyzing them one by one repeats tests and wastes the relationship among them. MANOVA tests the treatments on the set as a whole, and discriminant analysis looks for the combination of variables that best separates the groups. Both run in the browser, validated against R’s manova and summary.manova and against lda from the MASS package.

MANOVA

Choose the Response variables (2 to 10), the Treatment factors (up to 3, crossed), the Blocks (optional), the Main test and the Significance level. The main test is the one the conclusion uses: Pillai, the most robust and the default; Wilks; Hotelling-Lawley; or Roy, which gives an upper bound for F and an optimistic p. The MANOVA: the four tests table includes all four. The sums of squares are sequential (type I), as in summary.manova: the block enters first and then the factors, in the order of their columns in the table (not in the order in which you check them); with unbalanced data, that order changes the test of each term.

With four chili pepper varieties in three blocks, five fruits per plot, the variety changes the set of four measurements (Pillai = 2.668, F(12, 159) = 106.40, p < 0.001). The block is borderline: Pillai gives p = 0.063, and Roy, with its optimistic p, 0.005; that is why you should decide with the main test and not with the most favorable one. The traffic light checks that there are enough error degrees of freedom for the number of responses (green with at least three times as many; here, 54 for 4) and the equality of the covariance matrices with Box’s M test among the treatment combinations, without the blocks (χ²(30) = 30.13, p = 0.459). The other tables give the ANOVA of each response separately, Box’s M test group by group and the means of each factor; the plot shows the profile of the standardized means (with 12 levels or fewer).

4.22Power, replicates and sampling

Before the trial: how many replicates, how many plants or how many seeds you need to detect a difference that matters or to estimate with an acceptable error. Six of the eight methods of the Sample size, power and sampling family need no data. When data are loaded, they are in the catalog like any other method, with the No data needed mark; with an empty worksheet, the Calculate a sample size or power button of the Explore block enables them, and the catalog of the Analysis block then shows only this family.

The power calculations ask What do you want to calculate?: the sample size, the power of a given size or the effect that can be detected (the difference between means, the variation among the ANOVA means or the correlation; for two proportions, only the size or the power). They use the same formulas as R (power.t.test, power.anova.test, power.prop.test and pwr.r.test from the pwr package) and plot power against sample size. The Alternative hypothesis can be two-sided or one-sided; with a one-sided one, the app tests the positive direction, so the difference or the correlation must be written as positive numbers (with a negative number, the power comes out as 0% and the sample size makes no sense).

With a finite population, the mean is corrected with n = n₀/(1 + n₀/N) and the proportion with n = n₀/[1 + (n₀ − 1)/N], because the variance of a proportion carries the factor (N − n)/(N − 1). In the example of the table, n₀ = 322.7 and n = 237.7, which is rounded up to 238. The plot shows how many units are needed for each error: halving the error quadruples the sample when the population is large.

Estimating from a sample

The other two methods analyze a sample already taken, in the Variable measured in the sample. Simple random sampling estimates the mean or the proportion (Estimate: automatic, which gives the proportion if the column is 0/1) and, with the Population size (0 if very large or unknown), the total, with the finite population correction; its plot shows how the interval narrows as the sample grows. Stratified sampling also asks for the Strata (orchards, fields, regions, soil types) and the Population size of each stratum (N_h), one field per stratum with all the units in it, not only those sampled. With Allocate a new sample of (optional), it proposes how to allocate it among the strata: proportional and Neyman allocation.

With the avocado from three orchards (1,580 trees), the simple estimate gives a mean of 51.89 kg per tree (SE = 1.975) and the stratified one 54.32 (95% CI: 52.00 to 56.65), with a considerably smaller standard error (1.142) and 32.29 effective degrees of freedom (Satterthwaite). The difference has an explanation: the sample took 16 trees from Planicie, which has 57% of the trees and the most productive ones, and 20 from the other two orchards, which have 43%; the simple estimate weighs them equally and is pulled down, while the stratified one weighs each orchard by its size. That is why this example serves to compare the two methods, but the correct estimate for these data is the stratified one.

For a new sample of 60, Neyman takes 17 in Ladera, 29 in Planicie and 16 in Vega: more in the large strata and in the variable ones. Each stratum is rounded up, so the sum can exceed the total; when it does, the conclusion says so (“Rounded up, they add up to 62”). The How to allocate a sample of 60 table gives the unrounded values of the two allocations (proportional: 15.95, 34.18 and 9.87; Neyman: 16.14, 28.22 and 15.63) so you can adjust them by hand. If all the strata were measured completely (each Nh equal to the number sampled), it is a census: there is no sampling error, the SE is 0, the interval reduces to the estimated value, and the conclusion explains this.

4.23Survival analysis

When the response is the time until something happens (germinating, flowering, dying) and in some units it did not happen before the end of the trial, those units are censored: you know that their time is longer than the one observed, but not by how much. Averaging the days without them, or with them as if they had germinated on the last day, biases the result; survival analysis uses them correctly. Both methods were validated against R’s survival package.

Both ask for the Time to event (or to censoring) and the Event (1 = occurred, 0 = censored): 0 if the unit finished the trial without the event or was lost. The Strata (optional) (lots, sites, sowing dates) give each stratum its own baseline hazard, and the groups are compared within them. The Confidence level changes the intervals; the conclusions and the traffic light always decide with α = 0.05.

Kaplan-Meier curves

With 120 bean seeds in three treatments, checked every day until day 21, the median number of days to germination is 17 in the control, 5.5 with soaking and 4 with scarification, and the curves differ (log-rank χ²(2) = 37.89, p < 0.001). In all, 17 seeds (14.2%) did not germinate: they are censored, and the traffic light reports it (it turns amber if they are more than half).

The dashed line marks 0.5: where each curve crosses it lies its median. The marks at the end of the curves are the censored units.

The Groups to compare (optional) can be up to 12; without them, a single curve comes out, with its band. The options are the Curve interval (log, the one in survival; log-log; or linear) and, with groups, the Test to compare the curves: log-rank, with the same weight for all times, or Peto-Peto, which gives more weight to early times (with the bean, χ²(2) = 31.47); the strata come into play when you choose groups (stratified by lot, χ²(2) = 32.81). The tables give the median of each group with its interval and the 25% and 75% quartiles (a “—” when the curve does not go down that far, like the 75% of the control, which stays at 0.30), the table of the test (observed against expected events) and the survival table at each time with events.

Cox model

Cox’s proportional hazards model measures the effect of the Factors (treatments, varieties) and of the Numeric covariates on the instantaneous hazard of the event, as a hazard ratio (HR): when it is greater than 1, the event comes sooner. In the factors, each level is compared with the first. Tied times are handled with Efron (the one in survival, better with whole days) or with Breslow.

With the bean, the treatment and the seed weight, stratified by lot: scarification multiplies the hazard of germinating by 4.459 (95% CI: 2.628 to 7.568) and soaking by 2.096, relative to the control. The HR of the weight is 0.02058 per gram; since the seeds weigh about 0.4 g, it is better to read it per tenth of a gram: 0.020580.1 = 0.678, a third less hazard for each additional 0.1 g. The traffic light checks the proportionality of the hazards (like cox.zph: p = 0.753, no evidence that the effects change over time), that there are at least 10 events per coefficient (here, 103 events for 3 coefficients: 34.33) and that the fit converged. The tables include the three global tests and Harrell’s concordance C (0.690); the plot shows each HR with its interval on a logarithmic scale, with the line at HR = 1.

4.24Time series

A time series is a variable measured at equal intervals (every month, every week, every year), where nearby values resemble each other. The four methods ask for the Variable measured over time and the Time column (to order the series), and they were validated against R (acf, pacf, Box.test, decompose, arima and the trend package). The example is a weather station with the rainfall and temperature of each month from 2017 to 2024.

The time column is numeric (1, 2, 3…, or a period number) or of date type, and each time must appear only once; if the rows are already in order, — rows are already in order — is enough. The column only orders the series: the calculations assume equal intervals, so with dates there must be one per period (for example, one per month). Be careful with a years × months table converted to long format with Wide ⇄ long… (section 2.11): it ends up sorted by month and then by year, so “rows are already in order” would analyze a scrambled series. First create a continuous time column and use it as the time column; since the year usually ends up as a factor, the expression converts it to a number: (as.numeric(anio) - 2017) * 12 + mes gives 1 in January 2017 and 96 in December 2024. Autocorrelation and decomposition need the complete series, with no missing values; Mann-Kendall omits them and ARIMA keeps them in their place.

  • Autocorrelation: the ACF and the PACF with their band, and the Ljung-Box and Box-Pierce tests. With monthly data, it is best to set the Lags for the Ljung-Box and Box-Pierce tests (0 = 10) to 12 or 24; with 12, monthly rainfall is strongly autocorrelated (Ljung-Box Q(12) = 427.37, p < 0.001), with the annual cycle in plain view: positive at 1 month, negative at 6. The conclusion names only the first eight lags outside the band; lag 12 (0.76), that of the annual cycle, is in the table and in the plot. To check the residuals of an ARMA model, the Previously fitted parameters (fitdf) discount their degrees of freedom.
  • Decomposition: it separates the trend (moving average over one cycle), the seasonal component and the residual, additive or multiplicative (Type), with the Observations per cycle (frequency): 12 for months. In temperature, seasonality explains 96.01% of the variation without the trend. The plots show the series with its trend, the seasonal component and the residual.
  • Monotonic trend: the Mann-Kendall test (it does not assume normality) and Sen’s slope, resistant to extreme values, with the Slope confidence level. Temperature shows no trend over the eight years (τ = 0.058, p = 0.403; Sen’s slope of 0.008333 per month, 95% CI: −0.01364 to 0.02881).

As in survival analysis, the autocorrelation conclusions decide with α = 0.05; the chosen level changes the band.

ARIMA and forecasting

The ARIMA model describes the series with its past values (autoregressive, p), its past errors (moving averages, q) and the differences that make it stable (d), with its seasonal version (P, D, Q and the Seasonal period (1 = no seasonal part)). In Model order, you choose Set it manually and enter p and q, or ask to Search for the lowest AIC (p, q = 0…2; P, Q = 0…1), which uses the d and D you set and, with a period, tries 36 models in a few seconds. Without differences, the Series mean is estimated or omitted. Last come the Steps to forecast (up to 120) and the Forecast interval level.

With the rainfall, ARIMA(1, 0, 0)(0, 1, 1)12 leaves residuals without autocorrelation (Ljung-Box Q(22) = 15.46, p = 0.842): the traffic light is green. It is also the model the AIC search chooses with d = 0 and D = 1 (AIC = 739.14, the lowest of the 36). The forecast repeats the summer peak, close to 157 mm.

The lower limits of some dry months are negative (−10.33 mm in the first): the model does not know that rainfall cannot go below zero. To respect that, it is better to model a transformation of the variable (section 2.9).

The tables give the coefficients with their SE, the fit (σ², log-likelihood, AIC, BIC and the Ljung-Box test of the residuals, with 2 periods of lags if there is a seasonal part and 10 if not), the step-by-step forecast with its interval and, if the order was searched, the models compared by AIC. A second plot shows the autocorrelation of the residuals: if the model captured the structure, its bars stay inside the band.

8.1The criterion

A validation case is a dataset (from a book, a paper or an R package), a SigmaPro computation and R’s answer to the same computation. The case passes if every number in R’s answer matches SigmaPro’s:

|SigmaPro − R| ≤ 10−6 · max(1, |R|)

That is, six digits: absolute for numbers smaller than 1 and relative for larger ones. A few rules refine it: a tiny p value (smaller than 10−6) must also match in its relative digits, so that a p of 10−20 does not pass just for being “almost zero”; R’s missing values and infinities are expected as missing values and infinities; and text is compared exactly. Every key in R’s answer is compared, and every one of them must exist in SigmaPro’s result.

R’s answers are computed by R scripts kept alongside the app (tests/r/), with R 4.4.2 and the reference packages for each method (car, emmeans, lme4, survival, agricolae and others). SigmaPro does not use R to compute, except in the R engine methods (section 4.6): its core is its own, and R is the judge.

8.3Five classes of results

Not all numbers can be compared in the same way. Each case carries a class that says what kind of computation it is and how it is validated.

8.5The release rule

A method does not appear in the app until it has validation cases and all of them pass. The app checks this on its own when it opens, with the validation report it carries: if a case of a method failed, the method would disappear from the catalog and, if the assistant suggested it, the app would say that its validation has not passed yet instead of computing it. That is why everything this manual describes is validated.

8.6How to repeat the validation

The validation does not depend on whoever did it: with R and node installed, anyone can regenerate the references with their own R and compare again.

You need R (4.4 or later) with the packages each case requires, and node (20 or later; nothing needs to be installed with npm). From the SigmaPro folder:

Rscript tests/r/generar-todo.R      # regenerates the references with your R
node tests/motor-r.mjs              # the R engine methods, in the browser
node tests/correr.mjs               # compares and writes the reports
node tests/navegador.mjs            # the same in the browser, via file://
node tests/codigo-r.mjs             # the app's R code gives the same in R
node tests/exportar.mjs             # the exports, with independent readers

motor-r.mjs opens the app in the browser and downloads the R engine, so it needs internet; it runs before correr.mjs, which merges its cases into the report. correr.mjs writes the report that the Validation block shows and a version for publishing, in HTML and in CSV. The other checks of section 8.4 have their own programs in tests/: idioma.mjs, regresion.mjs, calidad.mjs, intro.mjs, fundamentos.mjs, glosario.mjs, orientacion.mjs, enlaces.mjs, suite-enlaces.mjs, sin-conexion.mjs, rendimiento/medir.mjs, rendimiento/lighthouse.mjs and accesibilidad/auditar.mjs; the two link checks need internet. The two external tools, the accessibility checker and the speed auditor, are installed separately, and an environment variable tells the tests where to find them: without the checker, the accessibility audit runs everything else and warns that that part was not done; without the auditor, the speed measurement does not run.

On Windows, tests/pruebas.ps1 finds R, node and the browser and runs the comparison against R, the equivalent R code, the English interface, the regression, the fact sheets, the quality of what you read, the browser test, the glossary, what guides you and the opening sequence. With -R, it first regenerates the references; with -Completo it adds the exports, privacy, performance and accessibility (about 15 minutes) and, if the speed auditor is installed, its measurement (about as long again); and with -Red, the checks that need internet: the R engine and the links of the app and of the suite.

9.6Formulas, language and offline use

The formulas in the fact sheets are mathematical text, not images: they can be read by a screen reader, enlarged without losing sharpness and copied.

SigmaPro writes them with its own converter to MathML, the formula language that browsers understand, without downloading any library. Each fact sheet is in Spanish and in English, with the same figures in both; you switch with the ES · EN selector like the rest of the app, and in English the notation follows the convention of that language (SE, SS and MS instead of EE, SC and CM).

The 78 fact sheets are app files (data/fundamentos/) that are loaded when they are opened. They work when you double-click index.html, in the published version and offline: the installed app stores them along with everything else. They also make up the methodological appendix of the exported report (section 7.2).

9.7How the fact sheets were reviewed

A fact sheet that describes something the app does not do is worse than no fact sheet at all. That is why each one was checked against the code and against the running app, in six independent reviews of 13 fact sheets each.

Each review checked the model, the hypotheses, the assumptions (and that the check and the alternative it proposes exist), the data and the example table, that the example runs and gives the figures in the write-up, the interpretation and the references (author, year, title, journal, pages and DOI). Of the 78 fact sheets, 49 changed in the review. Along the way, 18 errors in the app itself also turned up, from a calculation (weighted kappa when a rater does not use some category) to labels and messages; they were fixed, and the calculation errors have their new test case against R. The details are in docs/revision-fundamentos.md, and the list of analyses with the status of their fact sheet, in docs/inventario-analisis.md.

In addition, an automated test (node tests/fundamentos.mjs, part of the release battery of chapter 8) checks in every version that each analysis has its fact sheet with the nine sections in both languages, that the examples run, that the formulas convert and that no fact sheet names an option or a column that does not exist.

A group of experimental units that are similar to one another (a stretch of the field, a greenhouse, a working day) and that receives all the treatments; the variation between blocks is removed from the error.
BLUE
Best linear unbiased estimator: the adjusted mean of a genotype taken as a fixed effect.
BLUP
Best linear unbiased predictor: the predicted value of a random effect (a genotype, a block), shrunk toward the mean in proportion to how little is known about it.
Censoring
In survival analysis, a unit that reached the end of the trial without the event: its time is known to be longer than the one observed.
Coefficient of variation (CV)
The error standard deviation as a percentage of the mean; it measures the precision of a trial. In the Explore descriptives, it is the standard deviation of the variable as a percentage of its mean.
Comparison letters
In mean comparisons, two means that share a letter do not differ at the chosen α level; the letter a goes with the highest mean.
Confidence interval (CI)
The range of values for a parameter that are compatible with the data, at the chosen level (for example, 95%).
Covariate
A numeric variable that is not a treatment but explains part of the response (the number of plants, the altitude).
Degrees of freedom (df)
The number of independent pieces of information with which something is estimated; Satterthwaite and Kenward-Roger approximate them in mixed models.
ED50
In a dose-response curve, the dose that gives half of the effect between the limits of the curve; in the log-logistic model it is the parameter e. ED10 and ED90 give 10% and 90%.
Error stratum
Each unit size in a design with plots of several sizes (main plots, subplots), with its own error.
Factor and level
A classification variable (the hybrid, the dose as a treatment) and each of its values.
Fixed and random effect
Fixed: the levels were chosen on purpose and you want to draw conclusions about them. Random: the levels are a sample of many possible ones (blocks, locations, years).
Heritability
The proportion of the variation (among genotype means or among plots) that is genetic.
Interaction
When the effect of one factor depends on the level of another (one hybrid responds more to nitrogen than another). If it is significant, it is advisable to compare one factor within each level of the other.
Lack of fit
The part of the variation of the means of the replicated points that the model does not explain; if it is significant, the model does not have the right shape.
Marginal mean
The mean the model predicts for a level, with equal weights over the other factors; with covariates or unbalanced data, it is the one that gets published (adjusted mean).
Mega-environment
A group of environments in which the same genotype wins, repeatedly across years. With data from a single season, the sectors of the GGE biplot are possible mega-environments.
Odds ratio and hazard ratio
How many times more (or less) favorable an outcome is in one group than in another (odds), or how many times greater the instantaneous risk of an event is (hazards, in the Cox model).
Overdispersion
More variation than the assumed distribution allows (Poisson, binomial).
Power
The probability of detecting a difference that exists; it is set before the trial (usually 80%).
p-value
The probability of obtaining a result as extreme as the one observed, or more so, if there were no effect.
Relative risk
The ratio of the proportions of an outcome in two groups (for example, the germination of the treated seed divided by that of the untreated seed).
REML
Restricted maximum likelihood: it estimates the variance components after discounting the degrees of freedom spent on the fixed effects, and so corrects the downward bias of maximum likelihood.
Residual
What is left of each observation after subtracting what the model predicts.
Standard error (SE)
The standard deviation of an estimator: how much it would vary if the trial were repeated.
Traffic light
The box that says whether the assumptions of an analysis hold: green, fine; amber, check; red, problem; gray, not applicable or not enough data to check it. Not every analysis has one (section 4.5).
Type I, II and III sums of squares
Three ways of testing each term with unbalanced data: in sequence, adjusted for the terms that do not contain it, or adjusted for all the others (section 4.10).
Variance component
The part of the total variation contributed by a random factor.
